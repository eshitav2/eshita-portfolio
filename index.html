From export to equation

The problem

The spreadsheet detour

Reports showed customers their numbers, but not the ones they actually needed. To get a “gross profit margin %” row, they had to leave the product.

Today Open the report→ Export to a spreadsheet→ Rebuild the math by hand→ Share with stakeholders
With calculated fields Open the report→ Build the formula in the report→ Share with stakeholders
45%of QBO Advanced and IES customers export report data to spreadsheets, CSV or Excel to run their own calculations
48%of their time, by financial managers’ and advisors’ own estimate, goes to preparing reports and dashboards

“I want insights I can share with the right people. But QuickBooks limits how I customize reports and add KPIs. I’m not getting my money’s worth from reports.”The CFO job story that framed the project, in short form.

Research

What customers told us

I worked from moderated sessions on a clickable prototype with accountants, bookkeepers, advisors and finance leads, run in June and July 2025. Later concept tests on management reports showed the same pattern.

8 of 10 first-round participants raised custom formulas or reacted to them.

01

They already do this math elsewhere

Participants export to Excel for percentages, actual versus budget, overtime and bonus calculations. One reported around 25 hours a week on manual entry into websites and spreadsheets.

“Everything in QuickBooks has to be modified.”Accountant, later concept test

For the design

The need was real and recurring. Next step: catalogue the most common Excel calculations.

02

Ease depends on spreadsheet fluency

Easy for anyone who has used pivot tables or Excel formulas, hard for everyone else. One participant wanted videos or education to learn how to write a formula.

For the design

Feel familiar to Excel users while still guiding everyone else. Discoverability and help are part of the problem.

03

A blank formula box is a lot to ask

An accountant asked for ready-made formulas for the calculations she uses constantly: gross profit margin, net profit margin, percent of sales and percent of revenue.

For the design

Offer pre-built formulas as a starting point, alongside writing your own.

04

Formulas need time context

An accountant wanted budgeted sales for the full year against actual sales year to date. Row divided by row isn’t enough when the periods differ.

“I can see myself adding something like this year’s revenue minus last year’s revenue.”Small-business owner, later concept test

For the design

Let people choose the time period an input refers to.

05

Data isn’t always on one report

Several participants wanted values from multiple reports in one calculation. Advisors also wanted non-financial inputs such as employee counts and time-tracking data, though not every client needs them.

For the design

A scope boundary: the first release works from the report’s own rows and columns.

06

Trust and the finished output matter

A “valid input” confirmation drew an excited reaction. Printed client reports should hide calculated-field icons. Calculations on a “Display by” view could make a report too big.

“The report may get too big.”Accountant, on Display by

For the design

Validate as people type, keep prints clean, and treat wide views as a constraint.

07

Formulas should keep working next month

Advisors who report monthly want to set a calculation up once and have it update with the data, instead of rebuilding it for every period and every client.

For the design

A calculated row is a reusable definition, saved with the report and recomputed when the period changes.

Not everyone needed it. One participant called custom formulas “good to have” but didn’t expect to use them often, and two of the ten didn’t bring them up. So the first formula had to be quick to create, with no upfront learning.

Process

How the design evolved

I treated the work as a series of separate decisions and explored several options for each before converging. Tap a stage to see it.

Judgment calls

Four decisions that shaped it

1Give it a home: “Customize data”

Instead of adding another toolbar button, I evolved the existing Compare control into Customize data. Calculated fields became a section beside Time periods and Variances. The old “calculations” option inside Compare was renamed “Variances” to avoid a clash, and reports without Compare get the new control.

2Chips, not plain text

People choose values from a menu and type the operators. Each value becomes a colored chip that can be removed with its ✕ or backspace, so a formula reads as pulled-in data, not text that could be mistyped. A first-use tour with a live chip demo teaches this in one step. The formula font is deliberately custom, to signal that chips are values, not words.

Later concept tests on management report templates pointed the same way. When shown placeholder chips that pull in live values, participants reacted positively.

3Make the system do the checking

The equation is parsed from the operators used and verified, and the user is told if it isn’t valid. The report refreshes when the formula is saved. If someone later applies “Display by”, the formula carries over to the new columns, so a calculated row doesn’t silently break when the report’s shape changes.

4Extend the design system, on purpose

Two components went beyond what the design system offers: a typeahead menu with a suggested section and up to five levels of nesting, and formula chips with active and inactive states. I flagged these early as not yet system-compliant and noted that alignment with the design-system team was needed.

The solution

Find it. Build it. See it.

Users create custom rows and columns by building a formula from the report’s own rows and columns. It works on standard and custom reports. Custom rows can include a grand total, and custom columns can be added without a comparative period.

Reflection

Role, outcome and what I learned

My roleLead product designer. I owned the design end to end: framing the problem from the research, exploring where it starts and how formulas get built, prototyping, and walking the team through the flow before refining toward launch. I worked with product, engineering, research and content, and flagged early the two components that go beyond the design system so we could align with that team.
OutcomeCalculated fields launched to Early Access customers on IES. In research, 8 of 10 first-round participants raised custom formulas or reacted to them, and one accountant called calculations the most exciting part of the concept. People can now build custom rows and columns from a formula inside the report, on standard and custom reports, and share the result without the spreadsheet detour. Next, I want to measure how many customers stop exporting for their own calculations.
What I learned

Explore wide, then let go. I explored three trigger options and five ways to build a formula. Converging meant letting go of drafts I liked, and that made the final design better.

Design for the person who is not fluent. Suggested values and chips beat a blank box, and they keep the formula readable for everyone.

Let the system do the checking. Validating as people type, and carrying formulas over when the report changes, builds trust.

What I would design next. Letting people simply describe a calculation, and sharing calculated reports with the right people.

What’s next · a concept

What if you just asked?

Customers told me formulas feel risky and a blank box is a lot to ask. My next idea removes the box. Describe the calculation in a sentence, and AI writes the formula, asks when a term could mean two things, and shows its working so you can still edit it.
